## Dated findings

- **Ragusa and Venice.** The “Etymology and terminology” and “History” sections of [Quarantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quarantine) describe Ragusa’s 1377 thirty-day *trentino* and the Venetian Senate’s 1448 forty-day waiting period, which gave rise to *quarantena*. The article places these developments in sequence but does not state that Venice adopted or adapted Ragusa’s policy. The 1448 Venetian milestone remains as a separate root; the Ragusa-to-Venice proposal was removed. Venice’s 1403 lazaretto is another distinct event, but the article does not establish its lineage from Ragusa, so no edge or node is proposed.

- **Duncan, Liverpool, and national sanitation law.** The “Campaign work and acting on data,” “Liverpool Sanitary Act 1846,” and appointment sections of [William Henry Duncan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Henry_Duncan) state that Duncan submitted his Liverpool sanitary report to Chadwick’s Poor Law Commission in 1840; delivered and published mortality lectures in 1843; and that the pamphlet moved the Council to pass the local Act in 1846. The Act set housing rules, recognized need for a medical officer, and led directly to Duncan’s 1 January 1847 appointment. The article also says the locally promoted Act influenced drafting of the Public Health Act 1848. The 1840 report → existing Chadwick report, 1846 Act → existing Liverpool MOH, and 1846 Act → existing national act are proposals. The 1843 lecture → 1846 local Act edge is internal. No edge treats Liverpool’s urban conditions as an input.

- **London administration and drainage.** [Metropolis Management Act 1855](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolis_Management_Act_1855) and the “Creation” and “Sewage” sections of [Metropolitan Board of Works](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_Board_of_Works) date the Act to 1855 and the board’s assumption of powers to 1 January 1856. The board coordinated London infrastructure and created the core sewerage system, with much of the engineering work under Bazalgette. The Act → board edge is internal; board → existing London Main Drainage is a proposal. The Great Stink is described as a political trigger in the sewer history, but is not treated as a technical or institutional input.

- **New York health administration, laboratory service, and school nursing.** [New York City Department of Health and Mental Hygiene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_City_Department_of_Health_and_Mental_Hygiene) states that the state established the Metropolitan Board of Health in 1866 and replaced it with a Department of Health in 1870, adding street-cleaning and sanitary-permit duties. The board → department succession is proposed. [Hermann Biggs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Biggs) dates his pathology and direction of city bacteriology laboratories to 1892–1901 and says he applied bacteriology to infectious-disease prevention and control. The node is scoped to Biggs’s 1892 directorship, not an unsupported founding date for the laboratories; the 1870 department → laboratory edge is internal. [Henry Street Settlement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Street_Settlement) says the settlement funded the first public-school nurse in 1902. This is a specific application of Wald’s neighborhood nursing; the public-health nursing → school-nursing link is proposed. The earlier 1892 school medical-inspection milestone is not linked because the reviewed sources do not show that it enabled the nurse service.

- **Federal and British laboratory institutions.** The “Origins” section of [National Institutes of Health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Institutes_of_Health) dates the Marine Hospital Service’s Hygienic Laboratory to 1887 at Staten Island and its statutory redesignation as the National Institute of Health to 1930. The MHS → laboratory edge is proposed; the laboratory → 1930 institute edge is internal. The history of [Public Health Laboratory Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_Health_Laboratory_Service) says the national service was established under the NHS Act in 1946. Its separate account of the Emergency PHLS says existing laboratories were brought under its auspices in 1936; the overview [Public health laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_health_laboratory) instead says the Emergency PHLS was established in 1940. Neither article reconciles these as planning, formal establishment, or operational dates. I removed the disputed early node and its continuity edge rather than choosing a date. The clearly dated 1946 national service remains, currently without a parent.

## Prior-batch review proposal

The resolved [Health Organization section of Organisation of the League of Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organisation_of_the_League_of_Nations#Health_Organization) describes three components: the Secretariat’s Health Section, the Health Committee, and the General Advisory Council/Conference, with the OIHP handling most practical health questions. Its preceding account says the First Assembly in November–December 1920 considered the establishment of a new organization to deal with health; the detailed subsection itself gives no formal establishment date. The bibliography cites a specialist history titled *Coming to terms with world health: the League of Nations Health Organisation 1921–1946*. The [WHO history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Health_Organization) broadly says the League established its Health Organization when formed in 1920, but does not distinguish the League’s founding from the date of the health body’s formal organization.

The prior node `league-nations-health-organization` is therefore flagged separately for root review. The available archive does not establish that the formal Health Organization began in 1920. Safest options are to defer the dated node or replace it with a specifically scoped League health activity whose date and institutional status are supported. The bibliography’s 1921–1946 period is a lead, not by itself proof of a founding event; do not silently change the date to 1921.

## Integration correction: permanent League Health Organization

The resolved archive section describes 1920 discussions, while WHO's general article compresses those into a founding claim. A targeted official-source search was warranted by that conflict. WHO's [March 1963 World Health account](https://iris.who.int/bitstream/handle/10665/366781/WH-1963-Mar-eng.pdf?isAllowed=y&sequence=1) dates the organization to September 1923. A contemporary [Public Health Reports account, 21 August 1925, printed page 1736](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/68253/cdc_68253_DS1.pdf) describes Council approval on 7 July and Assembly approval on 15 September 1923. The search-indexed passages were available; direct PDF opening failed in the browsing tool. The existing stable ID now describes permanent establishment in 1923, and its League parent cites the contemporary account. Earlier planning/provisional activity is not represented as that same event. Its documented handover to WHO remains intact.

The Emergency PHLS remains deferred. An [MRC institutional history, printed page 7](https://www.ukri.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/MRC-120122-halfacenturyofmedicalresearch.pdf) describes a laboratory becoming its nucleus at the outbreak of war in 1939; [Public Health England's May 2018 historical issue, page 4](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5ae9e817e5274a702130de15/VU_issue_278_european_immunisation_special.pdf) gives 1940. This does not resolve the archive's additional 1936 claim, so no inferred timeline was added. The latter source separately supports the retained 1946 national-service milestone.
