Question: which measured lunar distances, predicted lunar tables, and portable timekeepers made longitude at sea practically determinable? ## Evidence and dates

All article-body research used the local archive `F:/Wikipedia/wikipedia_en_all_maxi_2026-08.zim`, UUID `50e94998-c1ec-b5e7-ec34-499f1d907c30`. Focused extracts are in `.cache/navigation-expansion-4/`; earlier cached Sextant, Marine chronometer, Board of Longitude, and Tobias Mayer entries are in `.cache/wiki-research/` and this cache.

- [Octant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octant_%28instrument%29), “Inventors” and “Hadley's reflecting quadrant”: Hadley and Godfrey independently developed the octant around 1730; the Sextant article dates the first implementation of the double-reflection principle to around 1731. Hadley's reflecting quadrant used a sighting telescope and paired mirrors to measure angular separation. The new 1731 node represents this octant, not a sextant.
- [John Campbell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Campbell_%28Royal_Navy_officer%29), “Astronomy and Brest”: around 1747 Campbell first used a Hadley quadrant to measure Moon-to-star angles. In 1756 he successfully sea-tested Tobias Mayer's lunar tables and reflecting circle, comparing them with the wooden Hadley octant. He found the circle too heavy and the octant more useful, though limited to 90 degrees and unstable in its wooden frame. Campbell recommended a 120-degree range and brass construction. [John Bird](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Bird_%28astronomer%29), “Life and work,” says Bird and Campbell designed portable sextants for sea; Campbell's article dates Bird's commissioned prototype to 1759. These findings support distinct 1747 observation, 1756 sea-trial, and 1759 sextant milestones.
- [Tobias Mayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tobias_Mayer), “Career,” records lunar tables communicated in 1752 and amended manuscripts submitted to the British government in 1755; Bradley compared the predicted lunar position with Greenwich observations. [Board of Longitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Board_of_Longitude), “Origins,” says Mayer's tables underlay lunar data in early Nautical Almanac issues. The 1755 node is the submitted manuscript set, not Mayer's later 1770 printed edition.
- [The Nautical Almanac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Nautical_Almanac), opening and publication history: the inaugural issue was for 1767 and included predicted lunar distances to facilitate longitude calculations. The issue year is not necessarily its printing year. The lunar-distance technique itself was fully published in 1763, so the new 1767 practice node means use with the first annual published tables, not invention of the method.
- [Lunar distance (navigation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_distance_%28navigation%29), “Method” and “In practice”: navigators measured the Moon's angle from a star or the Sun with a sextant, corrected for parallax and refraction, matched the result to predicted lunar distances to obtain Greenwich time, then compared Greenwich and local time to calculate longitude. The article describes later use of lunar observations to check a chronometer but gives no dated introduction. No dated check node is proposed.
- [John Harrison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Harrison), “Longitude watches” and “H4”: the Jefferys precision watch was made for Harrison around 1752–1753; it had temperature compensation and a miniature fusee maintaining power, and Harrison incorporated those features into H4. The H4 watch is marked AD 1759. A separate first transatlantic trial left Portsmouth aboard HMS *Deptford* on 18 November 1761; it reached Kingston in 1762 and was found about one nautical mile from the known longitude. The node records the trial's departure year and states the result date separately.

## Existing-node proposals and limits

The existing `sextant` should remain a sextant. Its current 1731 date describes the double-reflection principle and Hadley–Godfrey octant, while Campbell's and Bird's accounts date a brass marine sextant prototype to 1759. The proposal keeps the stable ID and title, dates the milestone to Bird's prototype, and replaces the broad optical-lens parent with the octant design, Campbell's informing sea trial, and the Board of Longitude's documented commission. The octant design remains an independent input despite its alternate path through the trial: retained double-reflection construction differs from the trial's evidence about range and frame stability. Integration omitted the proposed direct telescope edge because the general Sextant article's component description does not establish the precisely dated prototype's equipment. The checked telescope input to the Hadley octant remains.

The existing `marine-chronometer` should be scoped to Harrison's H4 and dated 1759, the date engraved on the instrument; its article reference is updated to John Harrison to match that checked scope. Its H4 component parent is the Jefferys precision watch, whose specific temperature-compensation and maintaining-power features H4 reused. The pendulum-clock parent is proposed for removal because the source says ship movement made pendulum clocks unsuitable at sea. The 1761–1762 Jamaica voyage is represented separately as an H4 trial, with the Board of Longitude as an enabler because it arranged the test. H3 is not retained as an H4 parent: the source describes H3's failed balance approach but does not establish a specific H3 component or lesson carried into H4.

The archive's Marine chronometer article calls the 1767 *Aurore* voyage the first chronometer longitude measurement, but the John Harrison article documents a successful H1 longitude sea trial in 1736 and the H4 transatlantic test in 1761–1762. The local *Aurore* article lookup did not resolve, and no source here identifies the vessel's actual chronometer maker. The *Aurore* claim is therefore deferred rather than retained as a universal first. Likewise, although lunar distances could check chronometers, no specific dated check was found; that undated use is documented here without assigning it the almanac's 1767 date.

These are focused secondary-source checks from one archive. They establish article support and source availability, not independent verification of the historical claims or review freshness.
