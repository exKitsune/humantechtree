## Question and scope

This batch adds dated machinery and processes for mineral extraction and beneficiation, textile preparation and finishing, and equipment or materials used in those operations. It contains 23 milestones in `data/production-expansion-2.json`. The local archive is `F:/Wikipedia/wikipedia_en_all_maxi_2026-08.zim`, UUID `50e94998-c1ec-b5e7-ec34-499f1d907c30`. The focused reader was `.cache/read-wiki.py`; article text and review excerpts are retained only under `.cache/production-expansion-2/`.

## Findings from the archive

The entries use specific attestations rather than broad origin claims. Roman mining sites attest stamp mills in the first and second centuries CE; `year: 100` is a representative approximate date. The same article describes water-powered cams and trip hammers and ore crushing. Modern hydraulic mining is separately dated to Matteson's California use in 1853. The patio process was used at scale by Bartolomé de Medina in Pachuca in 1554; the article notes debate over whether added copper-sulfate magistral was needed when ores already contained it. Gold cyanidation was patented by MacArthur and the Forrest brothers in 1887, with commercial use on the Rand by 1890.

The batch separates three later mineral operations by their documented stage. Merrill's zinc-dust precipitation process is dated around 1900 in the archive; Thomas Crowe's vacuum deaeration is described only as a later refinement, without a year, so this node is scoped to Merrill's change and does not claim the combined Merrill–Crowe apparatus existed in 1900. The ball-mill article reports flint grinding for pottery in 1870 and separately describes later ore-grinding use; the file records the reported 1870 implementation without asserting that this exact mill supplied a particular later flotation plant. The flotation article explicitly says the Elmore brothers' 1897 industrial plant agglomerated pulverized sulfides with oil and was *not* froth flotation. It dates the successful Sulman–Picard–Ballot agitation-froth process at Broken Hill to 1905; the node uses that narrower milestone. Frasch first brought molten sulfur to the surface in 1894. The electrorefining article distinguishes the 1847 experiment, Elkington's 1865 patent, and the first successful plant at Pembrey in 1870; the node is scoped to the commercial plant.

For textiles, the Carding article dates Lewis Paul's hand-driven carding-machine patents to 1748 and describes mill adoption during the 1780s. The Wool combing machine article dates Cartwright's first patent to April 1790; the Carding article explains that combing can follow carding to remove shorter fibers. Roller printing was patented by Thomas Bell in 1783 and entered Lancashire cotton mills in the 1790s. The Ring spinning article credits John Thorp's frame to 1828/29 and explicitly identifies it as descended from Arkwright's water frame. Mercer devised the caustic treatment in 1844; the better-known tension-induced luster was Lowe's later 1889 refinement. Perkin discovered mauveine in 1856 and opened a mass-producing dyeworks the following year. The Alizarin article dates the anthracene synthesis to 1868. Indigo dye's Chemical synthesis section dates Pfleger's N-phenylglycine route to 1901 and describes its alkaline-melt chemistry. Its Synthetic development section says BASF had an economically viable and commercially feasible process in use by 1897, while the Chemical synthesis section also calls the 1901 Pfleger route the first commercially practical route. Those article statements conflict; this entry covers only Pfleger's specific 1901 route and claims no first-commercial priority.

For machinery and explosive materials, the archive dates Sobrero's discovery of nitroglycerin to 1846 and Nobel's dynamite patent to 1867. Albert's wire rope was developed for Harz mine hoists between 1831 and 1834. Davy presented his gauze-screen lamp in 1815; the article also documents Clanny's earlier lamp and Stephenson's independent work, so this entry names Davy's design without claiming sole priority for safety lamps. The Steam hammer article dates the first working machine to 1840 and records the Bourdon/Nasmyth dispute; the node describes the operating capability. The Drifter drill article dates Trevithick's first steam drill to 1813 and distinguishes it from later powered rock drills.

## Relationship decisions and limits

The authored parents represent immediate inputs supported by these article descriptions: water-wheel power for the water-powered stamp mill; cyanide leaching for Merrill's zinc-dust recovery; the water frame as the explicit design lineage for ring spinning; woven cotton as a substrate for mercerization; nitroglycerin as dynamite's explosive charge; drawn wire as wire-rope and lamp-gauze material; and boiler-produced steam for Trevithick's steam drill and the industrial steam hammer. Forging remains an independent direct-operation input to the hammer. The steam-engine node describes an earlier atmospheric pumping machine, so it was not used as the immediate power input; the generic drill node describes rotating points, so it was not treated as a direct ancestor of the percussive steam drill. The wool-combing machine remains a root because the archive establishes a generic process sequence from carding to combing, not that a mechanized carding machine contributed to Cartwright's 1790 implementation. Copper electrorefining is left without parents because the dated plant source establishes electrolysis but not which particular generator or electrolysis-law milestone contributed to it. Mineral processes without a specific documented prior capability in the baseline remain roots rather than receiving generic mining, metallurgy, or chemical parents.

Before this scope pass, the combined audit flagged only forging -> steam-hammer for an alternate path. That edge remains because forging is the operation performed by the hammer, an independent direct role from its steam supply; its directContribution field records that distinction. After replacing the atmospheric steam-engine parent with the direct steam-boiler input and removing the unsupported drill and carding-machine links, the final read-only audit returned no candidates.

A possible incoming edge from the new `carding-machine` to the existing `water-frame` is recorded separately in `.cache/production-expansion-2/incoming-links.md`. The Water frame article says Arkwright's final mill combined the frame with a carding machine for continuous processing, but the existing node names the spinning frame itself; integration should decide whether to broaden that node's target scope before adding the relationship.

## Deferred or rejected candidates

- `Bayer process` was not added: the archive article supports the refining chemistry and function but did not provide a dated implementation/attestation in its focused text. No year was inferred from outside the evidence gathered here.
- The `Washoe process` article resolved in the archive but yielded no usable article prose from the helper, so no date or edge was authored.
- Existing `water-frame` and `electric-arc-furnace` nodes were checked and not duplicated. Other obvious textile machinery already in the baseline includes the flying shuttle, spinning jenny, spinning mule, power loom, Jacquard loom, and cotton gin.
- The Elmore 1897 oil-agglomeration process was not represented as froth flotation. The later Crowe vacuum improvement was not backdated to Merrill's circa-1900 patent. Broad parent links based only on a market, application need, or later use were omitted.

Archive title resolution and article statements do not independently establish every edge or replace specialist historical review. Several root entries still need more specific precursor research if future work seeks to connect them.
