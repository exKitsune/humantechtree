## Findings and decisions

- **Amsterdam Wisselbank (`bank-amsterdam`, 1609):** *Bank of Amsterdam*, “Tasks” and lead. It handled a wide variety of coins, stable bank money, and book-entry domestic and international settlements. The record is scoped to this city-backed payment and currency-stabilization institution; it does not label the Wisselbank a central bank, since the archive notes scholars disagree and it lacked several later central-bank functions. The retained money edge records circulating coin and the stable account unit as inputs to settlement. A proposed trade edge was removed because the cited passage describes the bank settling trade payments, not trade enabling the bank.
- **London bankers' clearing house (`bank-clearing-house`, by 1770):** *Clearing house (finance)*, “Bank clearance.” London bankers' clerks met to exchange cheques and bills payable at their houses; centralized daily exchange replaced individual clerks visiting every bank. The archive dates its establishment to a range between 1750 and 1770, so 1770 is a conservative latest-by locator, not a claimed exact founding day.
- **FDIC established by the 1933 Banking Act (`us-federal-deposit-insurance`, 1933):** *Deposit insurance*, “History in the United States,” cross-checked with *Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation*, “Establishment of the FDIC: 1933.” The Act created the FDIC on June 16, 1933; the article distinguishes this authorization/establishment from 1934 deposit-insurance limits and operation. The node marks establishment, not a claim that coverage began in 1933 or that this was the first guarantee experiment worldwide.
- **BankAmericard network (`bankamericard-network`, 1958):** *Credit card*, “BankAmericard and Master Charge.” Bank of America launched a third-party bank-issued revolving-credit network accepted by many merchants, addressing the two-sided acceptance problem of earlier merchant-specific cards. Earlier general-purpose charge cards are not equated with this revolving-credit model. The money edge was removed: its rationale described the card’s function, rather than an evidenced input distinct from bank-provided credit.
- **Fedwire (`fedwire`, 1918):** *Fedwire*, “History.” A Federal Reserve telecommunications system linked all twelve Reserve Banks and the Treasury by telegraph using Morse code to move funds. Parents name the banking operation and the actual transmission methods.
- **Automated clearing houses (`automated-clearing-house`, 1968):** *Automated clearing house*, “History.” The archive identifies UK BACS as the first system to process payments in April 1968 and the first U.S. ACH in 1972. The node marks the network category's first cited operation, rather than claiming a single U.S.-only origin. No edge to the older London clearing house was added: the article establishes functional similarity but does not document direct lineage.
- **Corporate university degrees (`academic-degree`, approximate 1180):** *Academic degree*, lead. The archive says Bologna first awarded a Doctor of Civil Law in the late twelfth century. The year is a locator for that cited late-century milestone. The article's competing theories about links to Islamic licenses remain unresolved; the record makes no lineage claim.
- **Research doctorate (`research-doctorate`, 1810):** *Doctor of Philosophy*, “Educational reforms in Germany.” The Berlin university reforms tied a final arts-faculty qualification to a dissertation showing research contribution; the model later spread to the United States. The entry is the research-degree form, not every medieval doctorate.
- **Normal schools (`normal-school`, 1685):** *Normal school*, lead and “Europe.” The archive describes teacher-training schools and identifies La Salle's École normale at Reims as generally considered the first. It does not establish a direct predecessor in the current graph, so this node has no parent rather than an invented one.
- **Monitorial instruction (`monitorial-instruction`, 1796):** *Monitorial system*, lead and “Lancasterian system.” The archive says Bell observed pupil-teacher practices in Madras from 1789 to 1796 and developed a system where more advanced pupils taught others; it spread as a low-cost way to extend primary instruction. The year locates the end of the documented observation period. No existing parent records that specific Madras practice, so none was inferred from the article's links.
- **Jesuit Ratio Studiorum (`ratio-studiorum`, 1599):** *Ratio Studiorum*, lead. A set of regulations standardized a globally influential curriculum for Jesuit officials and teachers. `society-jesus` directly organized the school network; the record does not imply Christian institutions were prerequisites for education generally.
- **Laboratory-oriented chemistry teaching (`laboratory-oriented-teaching`, 1825):** *Justus von Liebig*, lead and “Research and development.” The archive says Liebig devised modern laboratory-oriented teaching as a Giessen professor and records his full chemistry appointment in December 1825. The year marks the documented appointment with laboratory allowance; the method's precise start is not stated. The university setting is the only parent.
- **Royal Society (`royal-society`, 1660):** *Royal Society*, lead and “Founding and early years.” The learned society formed from recurring groups of natural philosophers, including meetings at Gresham and Wadham, and organized experiments and papers. The university edge is specifically supported by natural philosophers meeting at Wadham College, part of Oxford University; Gresham College is not used as a university parent. Experimental inquiry remains a separate influence. The Society is classified under education as a knowledge institution.
- **Philosophical Transactions (`philosophical-transactions`, 1665):** *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society*, lead and “Origins.” The first issue appeared in March 1665; it was the second journal devoted exclusively to science. It provided registration, dissemination, and archiving, and was published by the Society's secretary under Society council rules. The journal is represented as a specific publication, not as the origin of all journals.
- **Northcote–Trevelyan Report (`northcote-trevelyan-report`, 1854):** *Northcote–Trevelyan Report*, lead. It recommended merit-only examination-based entry and a permanent politically neutral service, and was influenced by Chinese imperial examinations. The record describes the recommendation only.
- **British merit-based civil-service recruitment (`british-merit-civil-service`, 1870):** *Civil Service (United Kingdom)*, history section. The Civil Service Commission began overseeing open recruitment in 1855; the source identifies Gladstone's 1870 Order in Council as the decisive implementation of the report's proposals. This separates the design from actual adoption.
- **French Council of State established (`french-administrative-court`, 1799):** *Conseil d'État*, lead and history section. The Consulate established it in 1799 as an executive council that advised on law and adjudicated claims against the state. The Restoration retained it as an administrative court; the source says its role was more precisely defined by an 1872 act. The node title and summary describe the 1799 establishment and do not imply the later court’s independent status. No earlier graph node directly explained this French legal arrangement, so no parent was supplied.
- **Peace of Augsburg (`peace-augsburg`, 1555):** *Peace of Augsburg*, lead and overview. The treaty gave Lutheranism legal status in the Holy Roman Empire and set a limited confessional choice for territorial rulers. The parent is the Protestant Reformation, whose conflict created the settlement's immediate problem. This is a scoped imperial arrangement, distinct from the later Peace of Westphalia.
- **Nicene Creed (`nicene-creed`, 325):** *Nicene Creed*, lead and history. Adopted at Nicaea to resolve a doctrinal controversy, the creed became a defining statement for traditions adhering to Nicene Christianity. The parent is Christianity because the text articulates doctrine within that community; it is not framed as a universal prerequisite for institutions.

## Rejected and deferred candidates

It reports Makdisi’s ninth-century dating of a specific teaching-law license as part of a contested doctorate-origin theory; that does not support assigning the general practice an approximate year.

- Do not add an Ottoman “millet system” as an established medieval institution: *Millet (Ottoman Empire)* says the arrangement was not systematic before the nineteenth century and that later origin stories were retrospective.
- Existing graph nodes already cover the first-pass candidates `hanseatic-league`, `imperial-examination`, `nalanda`, `madrasah`, `al-qarawiyyin`, `al-azhar`, `sankore`, `waqf`, `bill-exchange`, `double-entry`, `joint-stock-company`, `magna-carta`, `bill-rights`, `peace-of-westphalia`, `civil-code`, `khalsa-community`, `sufi-devotional-practice`, `edict-nantes`, `habeas-corpus`, `kindergarten`, `society-jesus`, `justinian-code`, `equity-jurisdiction`, and `house-wisdom`; no duplicate entries were created.
