## Supported milestones and relationships

- `pan-amalgamation` (1609): the Pan amalgamation article's opening/history states that Barba introduced the faster Potosí process, using heated shallow copper vessels with ore, salt, and mercury, sometimes copper sulfate; treatment fell from the patio process's weeks to 10–20 hours. The supplemental `patio-process -> pan-amalgamation` edge records the article's direct comparison and process-improvement context. The source gives patio use from 1557, while batch 2 dates Medina's Pachuca implementation to 1554; these are different reported attestations, not silently reconciled origin dates.
- `washoe-amalgamation` (1860s, represented as 1860): the article's “Washoe process” section calls it a variation of pan amalgamation and describes the iron tanks, mechanical agitation, mercury/salt/bluestone, and rotating muller. It therefore has `pan-amalgamation` as its specific process predecessor. The year denotes the documented decade, not an exact invention day.
- `reese-river-silver-process` (1879): “Reese River process” says Stetefeldt found the salt-roasting conversion in 1869 and that the method was introduced in the district in 1879. The node dates the implementation, and links from Washoe because it responds to ores the Washoe method did not handle well while retaining amalgamation pans. The summary does not collapse discovery and district introduction into one date.
- `paul-wyatt-roller-spinning` and `marvels-mill` (1738, 1742): “Cotton mill,” “Paul-Wyatt mills,” dates the Paul–Wyatt patent to 1738 and lists Marvel's Mill as operating from 1742, the first water-wheel-powered cotton mill. The new mill is linked to the particular spinning machinery it housed and to the water-wheel power source. The 1738 machine remains a root: the focused evidence did not establish a specific earlier machine input.
- `cromford-integrated-cotton-mill` (1771): the “Richard Arkwright” article’s “Cromford Mill” section explicitly dates the water-powered mill and says it covered both carding and spinning operations. It provides a dated account for both inputs. The article does not identify the exact carder installed there; this edge records the mechanized carding operation, not a claim that Walton’s later card or Arkwright’s 1775-patented design was already installed.
- `dolobran-wool-carding-mill` (1789): “Carding” dates the first Welsh carding factory to Dolobran and says it produced yarn for Welsh flannel. The entry connects that specific mill use to the mechanized carder capability.
- `copperplate-textile-printing` (1770) and `parkinson-multicolor-calico-printing` (1785): “Roller printing on textiles” says Bell practiced copperplate textile printing from 1770; his 1783 roller patent sought to reduce its cost. The incoming copperplate-to-roller edge is therefore proposed as direct influence. The same article says Bell's machine, after an improvement associated with Parkinson, was successfully used at Bamber Bridge to print calico in two to six colors in one operation in 1785. The 1785 node links to the roller-printing capability; it does not claim certainty about Parkinson's precise mechanism.
- `arkwright-carding-engine` (1775), `walton-practical-wire-card` (1834), and `walton-standard-carding-system` (1857): “Richard Arkwright,” “Carding,” dates Arkwright’s carding-engine patent to 1775, describes it as an improvement to Lewis Paul’s 1748 machine, and gives its roller/bobbin-and-flyer operation. “Carding” dates Walton’s 1834 machine and layered cloth-and-rubber card; it says their combination became standard and was manufactured by James Walton & Sons from 1857. Walton’s milestone is scoped to this exact later card-and-machine configuration, not first use of a wire-covered card. The article also notes 11 Spen Valley card-clothing factories by 1838, but that regional count is deferred: it does not establish a specific input enabling the factories, and generic machinery demand alone is not an authored edge.

## Inference limits and rejected candidates

The process-to-implementation links above are supported by statements that the named machinery was installed, housed, used, or retained as a variation. The Cromford source establishes mechanized carding at the 1771 mill but leaves the specific carder unidentified; that uncertainty is reflected in the node scope. “Foundation” and “enabler” labels summarize those documented roles; they do not establish that an innovation had only one cause. The Washoe year is a decade representation, and the mill dates are article-level historical attestations, not independently checked against archival records.

No new links were authored from ball mill to flotation, copper electrorefining to a general generator/electrolysis discovery, hydraulic mining to generic water machinery, the Frasch process to earlier sulfur-using applications, mauveine to aniline-synthesis routes, synthetic alizarin to coal-tar anthracene extraction, or Pfleger indigo to a broadly commercial aniline route. The consulted archive material establishes possible process/feedstock relationships in some cases, but not the precise historical input into the dated implementation. The carding-process-to-Cartwright wool-combing-machine link remains deferred: process sequence alone does not show that a mechanized carding machine contributed to that machine's implementation. Ball-mill use for flotation remains unproven despite flotation's generic need for finely ground ore.

A new read-only redirect-resolving reader showed that “Washoe process” is a section redirect to “Pan amalgamation,” not a source with no prose as the earlier batch-2 note reported. The section is now cited above. Integration may update that earlier research note's deferred-source sentence; no batch-2 file was edited here. The prior synthetic-indigo note's internal source conflict remains: “Chemical synthesis” credits Pfleger's 1901 route as first commercially practical, while “Synthetic development” reports BASF commercial feasibility/in-use by 1897. This batch makes no priority claim and does not use that disputed relation.
